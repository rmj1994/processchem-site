Reaction

About

Enter the reactants and products of a reaction you already know; balances it, applies your excess and conversion, and reports the outlet amounts with the heat of reaction and the equilibrium constant.

What This Models

A reaction you specify. You name the reactants and the products, and the coefficients follow from conserving every element — you never type them. You then anchor the calculation on one reference compound's amount, say how much excess the others carry, and the result is the full material balance together with the heat and the equilibrium constant.

This is the opposite of the Chemical Equilibrium mode. There you give a feed and the thermodynamics decides what forms. Here you have already decided what forms, and supply the conversion yourself. If you want to know whether your conversion is attainable rather than assume it, use Chemical Equilibrium.

Inputs

The form reads top to bottom as the equation, the compounds, the basis and the conditions, with Calculate pinned at the bottom.

Equation

The Equation card at the top shows the balanced equation and updates as you type, with a check line under it, element by element: "✓ Balanced · Ca 1 = 1 · C 1 = 1 · O 3 = 3". A typo or a set that cannot be balanced shows straight away in amber, with the reason, rather than after you press Calculate. Half a name is the normal state of a form being filled in, so the card stays quiet until the names resolve.

If the set can be balanced more than one way, the card says so — "This set balances more than one way. Choose how the shared element divides" — and offers the pairs of products that trade against each other as chips, with a percentage for each ratio: with carbon burning to both CO and CO₂, say, "% as CO (of CO + CO₂)", the share of the carbon that ends up as CO. It appears only when the balance is genuinely ambiguous, and only for pairs that settle it.

Reactants And Products

Reactants and Products each have a card with a row per compound, up to 6 of each: a name (suggestions appear as you type) and a phase badge, g, l or s. Tap the badge to step it through gas, liquid and solid. + Add reactant and + Add product add a row; ✕ removes one. The default is calcination: one calcium carbonate solid giving calcium oxide (solid) and carbon dioxide (gas).

The reference compound's row carries its amount, with a unit button. The unit menu is grouped by kind: Moles, Mass, Volume, Molar flow, Mass flow, Volumetric flow and Normal flow. Picking another unit of the same kind converts the number; picking another kind changes the basis for every amount on the form. A flow basis makes every result a rate.

Basis

I have this feed or I need this product (see The Two Ways To Anchor It). The reference compound is a reactant for a feed you have, and a product for a production you want, because only one side can be the thing you supply. Where there is more than one to choose from, Amount given for offers them as chips, and the amount moves to that compound's row.

Two chips sit under the switch. Excess or extra amounts opens the per-compound amounts and the inerts (see Stoichiometric Or Not). Not all of it reacts opens Conversion of the limiting reactant, in per cent. Left off, the reaction goes to complete conversion.

Conditions

Feed enters at is Reaction temperature or Its own temperature. At the reaction temperature, the default, the feed is taken to be already there and the duty is the heat of reaction alone. Its own temperature opens a Temperature for the feed, 25 °C by default, and the duty then includes the sensible heat.

Products leave at gives the reaction Temperature and Pressure, 800 °C and 1.01325 bar by default. Press Calculate. The results open on their own screen; Edit on the Inputs strip takes you back.

The Two Ways To Anchor It

I have this feed takes the amounts you enter and works out what comes out. I need this product works backwards: you name the production you want and it sizes the feed to deliver it at your conversion. If an amount you entered outright cannot reach the target, it says so and gives the most that can be made, rather than quietly delivering less.

Stoichiometric Or Not

The Excess or extra amounts chip decides how much the form asks of you. Left off — the usual case — the reaction is taken as exactly stoichiometric: every reactant is present in precisely the ratio the balanced equation calls for, nothing is in excess, and no inert is carried. Per-compound amounts have nothing to say under that assumption, so they are hidden rather than left to be filled in.

Turn it on to describe a real feed instead: an excess of one reactant, a product already present, a compound in short supply, or a diluent passing through. Each compound other than the reference gets an amount field, and an Inerts list appears in the Products card. The two sections below describe them. Anything typed there applies only while the chip is on — turning it off returns the calculation to pure stoichiometry rather than quietly keeping the last numbers entered.

Excess And Actual Amounts

These apply when Excess or extra amounts is on. Every compound except the reference can be given either way: tap the small Excess / Amount button beside its field to switch. Excess is a percentage over what the stoichiometry requires, so 20 % excess air means 1.2 times the oxygen the reaction needs, and 0 % means exactly enough. The excess is not consumed, so it appears in the outlet — that is what makes it visible in the masses.

An actual amount is taken as entered instead, in the same unit as the reference. That means a compound can be put in short supply, in which case it becomes the limiting reactant and the reference is no longer fully converted. The result names the compound that limited it and gives its conversion; the Stream table shows how much of every compound is left.

Inerts

Also only when Excess or extra amounts is on, an inert being by definition material beyond the stoichiometry. Anything that is present but does not react — the nitrogen in combustion air, a steam diluent — belongs in the inerts list, not among the reactants. Add one with + Add inert (e.g. N₂), up to 3, each with a name, a phase badge and an amount. It passes through untouched and takes no part in the balance, but it still counts toward the totals, which is what it is there for.

Phase Matters

Each compound carries the phase on its badge, and it changes the answer. Burning methane to liquid water releases about 88 kJ per mole more than burning it to steam: that is the difference between a fuel's higher and lower heating value, and it is entirely down to which phase you pick.

What comes back

The Inputs strip repeats the equation and the reference amount, for example "CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ · 5 mol calcium carbonate".

The headline card gives the Heat duty (a power on a flow basis), with a line saying whether the reaction "Needs heat: endothermic" or "Releases heat: exothermic", and which compound was the limiting reactant. Under it an energy diagram draws the reactants and the products as two levels with ΔH between them, "energy taken in" or "energy released".

Three tiles follow: ΔH and ΔG, per mole of reaction as written at your temperature, and K_eq, the dimensionless equilibrium constant, with a word on what it means: "goes to completion", "goes partway" or "hardly goes". Tap a unit to show the value in another.

The Stream card lists every compound, inerts included, with In (the feed) beside Out (the outlet), each a share of the stream and an amount, and a Total row. The Share menu above the table switches between mol % and mass %; the Amount menu takes any amount or mass unit.

The Reaction card then gives:

Row What it shows
Limiting reactant The reactant that runs out first.
Extent How many moles of reaction occurred (a molar flow on a flow basis).
ΔS (reaction) The entropy change, in J/(mol·K).
ΔH° at 298.15 K, ΔG° at 298.15 K The standard values, for checking against tables.
Conversion The conversion of the limiting reactant.

A note says what ΔH is per mole of the reference compound, that K depends on temperature alone, and whether the duty includes sensible heat. The next line names where the thermodynamic data came from, and Methods used shows the same per species. Amber banners flag anything that weakens the result: a compound entered in a phase it does not hold at your temperature, a heat capacity used beyond the range it was fitted to, a reaction whose equilibrium favours the reactants (so the conversion is yours and not one the thermodynamics allows), and gas volumes above about 10 bar, where the ideal molar volume is approximate.

Method

Each compound's enthalpy and entropy at temperature come from its standard enthalpy of formation, its absolute entropy and its heat capacity, exactly as in the Chemical Equilibrium mode. The reaction's own properties are the sums of those, weighted by the balanced coefficients νᵢ.

Key Relations

ΔH_rxn = Σ νᵢ·hᵢ            heat of reaction, products minus feed
ΔG°_rxn = Σ νᵢ·gᵢ           at standard-state activities
ln K = −ΔG°_rxn / (RT)      equilibrium constant

νᵢ is positive for a product, negative for a reactant, and the reported heat duty is this same sum applied to the whole outlet and feed: enthalpy of the outlet less enthalpy of the feed. With the feed entering at the reaction temperature the duty is the heat of reaction alone; give it its own temperature and the duty includes the sensible heat as well.

About The Equilibrium Constant

K depends on temperature alone. Pressure shifts where a real equilibrium settles, but it does not change K, and the value shown will not move when you change the pressure. Pressure does set the gas volumes.

What It Refuses

A set of compounds that cannot be balanced, or that can be balanced in more than one way where no choice of split settles it — the message says how many independent reactions the set admits, and which compound to remove. Ions are refused too: the balance conserves elements but not charge. So is a compound entered twice in the same phase, one that cancels out of the equation, or one that balances only on the other side.

Limitations

One reaction at a time, with no kinetics and no reactor sizing. Gas volumes use the ideal molar volume RT/P, which is consistent with how the activities are modelled but approximate above about 10 bar. Heat capacity correlations extrapolate silently, so a compound entered in a phase it does not hold at your temperature is flagged rather than corrected.
