Chemical Equilibrium

About

Enter any set of components and amounts; finds which compounds are most stable at a chosen temperature and pressure, with the composition, phases and heat duty.

What This Models

Chemical reaction equilibrium. You give a feed — any components, in mass or moles — and the calculation finds the combination of compounds with the lowest total Gibbs energy at your final temperature and pressure, conserving every element.

This is a different question from the other Thermo modes. Phase Equilibrium holds the chemical identity fixed and moves temperature, pressure and energy around; Reaction takes the reaction and the conversion from you. Here the species turn into one another and the answer is a composition.

Inputs

A Set-up picture heads the form, showing the feed going in, the equilibrium conditions, and the products coming out. Under it are three cards, Feed, Species allowed to form and Conditions, with Calculate pinned at the bottom.

Feed

Each row is one component: its name in the first box (suggestions appear as you type) and its amount in the second. + Add component adds a row, up to 8, and ✕ removes one. The default is 5 mol of calcium carbonate with 1 mol of nitrogen.

The unit button beside each amount opens a menu grouped by kind: Moles, Mass, Volume, Molar flow, Mass flow and Volumetric flow. Every row shares that one unit. Picking another unit of the same kind converts the numbers you typed; picking another kind (kg after mol, say) changes the basis for the whole feed and keeps the numbers as typed. A rate makes every result a rate too, at the same time base as the feed: a feed in kg/h gives moles per hour, and the heat is reported as a power. The composition itself is unchanged by scaling every amount, so a flow only changes the labels.

Tap Scale to a total if you would rather enter a composition than absolute quantities: a total field appears, your amounts are treated as proportions and scaled to it, so 1 and 3 against a total of 100 kg mean 25 kg and 75 kg. The normalised split is shown under the field.

Species allowed to form

The app works out every compound your feed's elements could form that it has thermodynamic data for, and says which elements it found: "Suggested from the elements in your feed (C, Ca, N, O)". The species allowed are shown as chips under Gas, Liquid and Solid. Tap a chip's ✕ to leave that species out. Where a group holds more than a dozen, the rest fold under "+ N more".

Small molecules (up to 6 atoms) are allowed by default, because those are the species that actually appear at equilibrium: a large hydrocarbon is never an equilibrium product of combustion. Where the feed's elements form only a modest number of candidates (about 60 or fewer), the list is not cut by size at all and everything up to 20 atoms is allowed by default, because a cut would remove real condensed phases such as magnetite. Species above 20 atoms are never allowed by default, since a very large formula unit makes the solver fail to converge.

+ Add beside each group opens a sheet of that phase's other candidates, smallest first. Tap one to allow it. Its search box matches name, formula or CAS number; Show all reaches the larger species, and Small molecules only hides them again. Select all shown allows everything currently listed, so a search narrows it first and a huge list is never added by accident. Up to 120 rows are shown at once, with a count of how many more your search would narrow to.

Some phases have two descriptions, such as the ideal FeO and the real non-stoichiometric wüstite. Only one of the two can be allowed: adding one removes the other, and Select all shown skips the superseded one, because the solver would otherwise treat them as two coexisting substances. Changing the feed so that its elements change works out the list again; retyping a name or an amount keeps the species you chose.

Conditions

Feed enters at is As equilibrium or Its own T, P. As equilibrium, the default, takes the feed to be already at the equilibrium temperature, so the heat reported is the heat of reaction alone. Its own T, P opens a Temperature and Pressure for the feed (25 °C and 1.01325 bar), and the heat then includes the sensible heat of bringing the feed up.

Equilibrium at gives the Temperature and Pressure the equilibrium is solved at, 900 °C and 1.01325 bar by default. Press Calculate. The results open on their own screen; Edit on the Inputs strip takes you back.

What comes back

The Inputs strip repeats the feed and the conditions, for example "calcium carbonate 5 + nitrogen 1 mol · 900.0 °C · 1.01325 bara".

The headline card answers first: the feed component that reacts most, as "100 % of CaCO₃ reacts", or "Nothing reacts". Under it a line names the phases present and the totals, "Gas + solid · 11 mol out from 6 mol in".

Three tiles follow: the two main products formed, such as CaO formed and CO₂ formed, and the heat, Heat in or Heat out, as an energy for a quantity or a power for a rate. Tap the ⓘ on the heat tile to see the data source for each species: every Gibbs energy is built from an enthalpy of formation, an absolute entropy and a heat capacity correlation. Tap a unit to show the value in another.

Product gas, mol % is a bar chart of the gas at equilibrium, its six largest species, with traces reading "<0.1 %".

Then one card per phase, Gas phase, Liquid phase and Solid phase. Each lists the species with In (the feed) beside Out (at equilibrium), each a share of that phase and an amount. The menus above the table set the columns: Share in mol % or mass %, Amount in any amount or mass unit, and for the gas Partial p in any absolute pressure unit. A trace share reads "<0.01". Species you allowed that did not form are named in a line under the table, "… allowed, not formed"; a phase with nothing in it says "none at equilibrium". A species counts as present only if it is more than a billionth of the total, so a trace of ozone or atomic oxygen does not clutter an industrial result.

A note confirms the result was "Found by minimising the total Gibbs energy over the allowed species", and Methods used at the foot shows the method and the data each species came from.

Amber banners flag species left out for want of a complete heat capacity ("Left out for want of complete data", at the top of the results), and a feed component used as the ideal stoichiometric phase where a more realistic one exists. Species excluded as impossible at this temperature — a liquid below its melting point, or a solid above it — are named in a grey note, since that is the app doing the right thing.

Method

Each species' own Gibbs energy at temperature is built from its standard enthalpy of formation, its absolute entropy and its heat capacity, and the feed settles into whatever combination of species makes the total lowest, one balance per element.

Key Relations

Gᵢ(T) = Hf,298 + ∫Cp dT − T·(S°₂₉₈ + ∫(Cp/T) dT)     per species
minimise  Σ nᵢ·μᵢ   subject to one balance per element
μᵢ = Gᵢ + RT·ln(yᵢP)     gas — depends on how much is present
μᵢ = Gᵢ                  solid/liquid — pure phase, unit activity

Hf,298 and S°₂₉₈ are the formation enthalpy and absolute entropy at 298.15 K; the two integrals carry each up to your temperature. μᵢ is the chemical potential the minimisation actually balances element-by-element, numerically, not by hand. A condensed phase's μᵢ does not depend on how much of it is present, which is why a solid either survives or disappears entirely rather than settling at some intermediate amount — only a gas's does, through the yᵢP mixing term.

The heat duty is the full energy balance: enthalpy of the products at the final temperature, less enthalpy of the feed at its own temperature. It therefore includes both the heat of reaction and the sensible heat.

About The Initial Pressure

It does not affect this result, and the field is not pretending otherwise. Feed enthalpy is pressure-independent for an ideal gas and for a condensed phase, so an isothermal-isobaric equilibrium depends only on the final pressure. It is still recorded, for completeness alongside the other conditions on the sheet.

The Limitation That Matters Most

Only the species you allow can form. Gibbs minimisation finds the best combination of what it is given, so a species left out simply cannot appear, and its absence from the answer means nothing at all. If you expect a product and do not see it, check it is among the chips under Species allowed to form before concluding it is not favoured.

Metastable Products Will Not Appear

Equilibrium is the state reached once every reaction has run to completion, so anything that survives in practice only because it decomposes slowly is absent from the answer — correctly, and unhelpfully if it is the thing being designed for.

Sodium hypochlorite is the clearest case. A chlorine scrubber is built to make it, but OCl⁻ → Cl⁻ + ½O₂ releases about 94 kJ/mol, so at equilibrium bleach has all decomposed: feed Cl₂ and NaOH here and the answer is NaCl and oxygen. The chlorine removal it reports is real — the chlorine is indeed gone — but it says nothing about the hypochlorite yield, which is the number a scrubber is rated on. Peroxides and most partial-oxidation intermediates behave the same way.

For those, use the Reaction mode instead: it takes the stoichiometry and the conversion from you rather than deriving them, which is exactly what a kinetically controlled product needs.

Dissolved Species Are Modelled As Pure Phases

Every condensed species is treated as a pure phase at unit activity. In a solution it is not: caustic soda in water is dissolved ions, and its activity is far from one — a 17 % NaOH solution is about 5 molal. Results for aqueous systems are therefore qualitative. They will get the direction and the stoichiometry right and the amounts only approximately.

Other Limitations

Ionic species are excluded: the solver balances elements but not charge, so admitting an ion would let it create charge from nothing. There are no solution phases — no molten slag, no solid solutions — so a system that really forms a melt is modelled only approximately; this handles calcination well and clinkering roughly. There is no kinetics. And heat capacity correlations extrapolate silently, so compositions much above 1700 °C should be treated as indicative.
