Electrochemical Reaction

About

A reaction driven by electricity, or driving it. Balances the equation as the Reaction mode does, then reports the cell voltage, the electrical energy, the heat, and the current for a monopolar or bipolar stack.

What This Models

The thermodynamics of an electrochemical cell: what voltage a reaction needs to be driven at, or produces when it drives, and what heat goes with it. Enter the reaction exactly as in the Reaction mode and say how the cells are wired; the electrons it transfers are counted for you.

It works in both directions and tells them apart itself. A reaction with a positive Gibbs energy has to be driven — an electrolyser. One with a negative Gibbs energy drives — a battery or fuel cell. The equations are the same; only the wording changes.

Inputs

The form reads top to bottom as the reaction, how much, the cell and the conditions, with Calculate pinned at the bottom. Most of it is the Reaction form, and behaves as described on that page. The defaults differ, opening on water electrolysis, 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂, with 1 mol/s of water fed at 25 °C.

Equation is the balanced equation with its element-by-element check line, as on the Reaction page.

Reactants and products is folded under one row; tap it to open the compound rows, with their phase menus, + Add reactant and + Add product. The key compound carries a KEY tag.

Inerts is its own card, as on the Reaction page.

How much opens with Set by: Feed and conversion (the default) or Current and voltage.

With Current and voltage you give the Current (A, kA or MA) and the Voltage, which is for The whole stack or One cell. Reactants are supplied as needed: the current sets how fast the reaction runs, by Faraday's law, ξ = cells × I × efficiency / (z F) for a bipolar stack and I × efficiency / (z F) for a monopolar one. The results then lead with what is produced at that current, the power (current × voltage), the cell voltage and the energy per kg, then Where the voltage goes: a bar splitting each cell's voltage into the reversible voltage the reaction needs, the bath's resistance, the other losses and the rest, the electrodes' overpotential. Below it, the heat the cells make above the thermoneutral voltage, and what is lost outside them. A voltage too low to drive the electrolysis at that current is refused, naming the least it needs. At 1000 A and 1.9 V, water electrolysis uses 50.5 kWh per kg of hydrogen.

With Feed and conversion, How much asks the same four questions as the Reaction page's Amounts card: What do you know? (a feed amount or a product I need), the key reactant or key product and its amount with a unit button, each other reactant as Stoichiometric, Stoichiometric excess % or Fixed amount (and products already in the feed), and How much reacts?, followed by a one-line summary of the feed. See Reaction.

Cell opens with the line "Electrons are counted from the oxidation states of the balanced equation." Then:

  • Cells in the stack is a whole number, at least 1. The default is 1.
  • Current efficiency is the share of the charge doing the intended reaction, in per cent, greater than 0 and at most 100. At 100 % every electron makes product. Below it, extra current is needed for the same output, so the current and the electrical power scale by 100 divided by the efficiency, while the reversible voltage does not change.
  • Wiring is Monopolar (parallel) or Bipolar (series); see Monopolar And Bipolar.
  • Voltage losses (optional, with Current and voltage) opens the bath per cell (its Conductivity, the Electrode area and the Gap between the electrodes) and Other losses for the busbars and contacts, one voltage for the stack. The bath's resistance is gap ÷ (conductivity × area). Its voltage drop is the cell current × that resistance, and its heat stays in the cell. The heat of the other losses is released outside the cells. Leave them empty to see all the losses as one overpotential.

Conditions is What do you know?, then Reaction at, Feed enters at and Products leave at, as on the Reaction page. With Heat added, the electricity into the cells enters the energy balance with the heat: at the current and voltage you gave, or, set by the feed, at the reversible voltage. A cell at the thermoneutral voltage with no heat added leaves at its inlet temperature.

An impossible entry, such as zero cells or an efficiency of 120 %, is refused with a message saying what is wrong.

The Electrons Are Counted For You

The electrons are always worked out from the oxidation states of the balanced equation, then reported as a result. There is no field to type them in. Water balances as 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂: four hydrogens go from +1 to 0, gaining one electron each, while two oxygens go from −2 to 0, losing two each — four gained, four lost, so four transferred.

This matters because the number belongs to the equation as balanced, not to the half-reaction you remember. 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ is two electrons per molecule of hydrogen, and this equation makes two of them. Two electrons would give 2.46 V instead of 1.23 V — and 2.46 V looks perfectly ordinary, between chlor-alkali's 2.19 and magnesium's 2.48, so nothing downstream could have caught it. Counting from the equation removes that trap.

It declines rather than guessing in two cases. A reaction that moves no electrons at all — calcination, say — has none to count. And where the rules contradict themselves, as in a peroxide, where hydrogen at +1 and oxygen at −2 leave a molecule that does not add up to zero, it says so instead of returning a confident wrong number. The reaction's own result is still shown, with the message under it.

What comes back

The Inputs strip repeats the equation, the amount and the number of cells, for example "2 H₂O → 2 H₂ + O₂ · 1 mol/s water · 1 cell".

The headline card, Cell · reversible voltage, gives E_rev in volts and says which way the reaction goes: "This reaction has to be driven: it needs at least 1.229 V per cell" for an electrolyser, or "This reaction runs on its own: the cell gives up to … V" for a battery or fuel cell.

Cell voltage draws the scale between the reversible and the thermoneutral voltage, with a sentence on what happens between and beyond the two marks: for an electrolyser, between them the cell draws heat from its surroundings, and above the thermoneutral voltage the extra energy becomes heat.

Up to three tiles follow: Current (A, kA or MA) and Power (min.), the power at the reversible voltage, both only on a flow basis, and Energy per kg of the product (the reference if it is a product, else the first product), in kWh/kg, MJ/kg, kJ/kg or BTU/lb. A batch has no rate in it and so has no current or power; a note says to give the amount as a flow to get one.

The Cell card then gives:

Row What it shows
Electrons transferred The count, with the note "Counted from the equation".
Volts per electron The reversible voltage divided by the electrons. It is there as a check: a count that is double or half what the reaction needs gives a number an engineer recognises as wrong, rather than one that is merely unfamiliar.
Reversible voltage, Thermoneutral voltage E_rev and E_tn from the relations below.
Stack voltage The voltage across the whole stack, from the wiring.
Electrical work, ΔG (per mol of reaction) The electrical work, in kJ/mol, to run the equation once as written. It is not a total and not a power (see Per mole, per hour, per kg).
Reversible heat, TΔS (per mol of reaction) The heat exchanged per mole of reaction, labelled absorbed (an endothermic cell) or released.
Electrical energy (min.) for this amount On a batch basis only: ΔG × the moles of reaction ÷ the current efficiency, the least electrical energy for the amount you gave.
Wiring The cell count and whether it is series or parallel, on a flow basis.

Methods used shows the method and where each compound's data came from. The reaction at the foot folds away the reaction's own result — heat duty, ΔH, ΔG and K, the Gas, Liquid and Solid tables with the whole-stream totals, and the Reaction card — exactly as on the Reaction page; tap it to open. An amber note appears if the reversible voltage falls outside the 0.5–5 V that practical cells occupy.

Method

An electrochemical cell turns the Gibbs energy of a reaction into electrical work, or electrical work into Gibbs energy. Everything on the results screen follows from four relations: two from thermodynamics, one from counting electrons, and Faraday's law.

Key Relations

E_rev = |ΔG| / (z·F)        reversible voltage — the least an electrolyser needs (or the most a fuel cell gives)
E_tn  = |ΔH| / (z·F)        thermoneutral voltage — the cell neither heats nor cools
z·F·(E_tn − E_rev) = TΔS    heat exchanged reversibly, per mole of reaction
I = z·F·ξ̇ / η               Faraday's law — a rate of reaction is a current
P_min = I·V_stack = |ΔG|·ξ̇ / η    the least electrical power
  • ΔG, ΔH, ΔS are the reaction's Gibbs energy, enthalpy and entropy change at the reaction temperature, per mole of reaction as written, from the same data as the Reaction page.
  • z is the number of electrons transferred per mole of reaction as written, counted from oxidation states (see The Electrons Are Counted For You).
  • F = 96,485 C/mol is Faraday's constant, the charge on a mole of electrons.
  • ξ̇ is the rate of reaction, moles of reaction per second, from the amount you gave on a flow basis.
  • η is the current efficiency, as a fraction: below 1, part of the charge does something else, so more current is needed for the same product.

Why two voltages. ΔG is the part of the reaction's energy that has to be delivered as electrical work: no cell can run below E_rev. ΔH is the whole energy change. The difference, TΔS, is heat. An electrolyser running between E_rev and E_tn takes that heat from its surroundings and cools. Above E_tn every extra volt becomes heat in the cell. Real electrolysers run above E_tn because of overpotentials and resistance, which this page does not model (see What This Does Not Do).

Per Mole, Per Hour, Per kg

The results give the same energy three ways, and they are easy to mix up.

  • Per mole of reaction (kJ/mol): ΔG and TΔS in the Cell card. "One mole of reaction" means running the balanced equation once as written. For 2 AlCl₃ → 2 Al + 3 Cl₂ that is 2 mol of AlCl₃ consumed, 2 mol of Al and 3 mol of Cl₂ made. It does not depend on how much you make.
  • Per unit time (W, kW): Power (min.) = ΔG × the rate of reaction ÷ η. Only a flow has a rate, so only a flow gives a current and a power.
  • Per kg of product (kWh/kg): Energy per kg = power ÷ the product's mass flow, or ΔG ÷ (η × the product's mass made per mole of reaction). This is the figure usually quoted for an electrolysis process.

On a batch basis the Cell card adds the total for your amount: ΔG × moles of reaction ÷ η, in J, kJ or MJ.

Worked Example: Aluminium From Aluminium Chloride

1 kg/h of aluminium from solid aluminium chloride, 25 °C, 1.01325 bar, one cell, 100 % current efficiency, set as A product I need, aluminium 1 kg/h:

  1. Balance. 2 AlCl₃ → 2 Al + 3 Cl₂.
  2. Electrons. Aluminium goes from +3 to 0 and chlorine from −1 to 0. Two aluminium atoms take 3 electrons each, so z = 6 per mole of reaction.
  3. Rate of reaction. 1 kg/h ÷ 26.98 g/mol = 37.06 mol Al/h. The equation makes 2 Al per mole of reaction, so ξ̇ = 18.53 mol/h = 0.005148 mol/s.
  4. Gibbs energy. ΔG = +1,257 kJ/mol and ΔH = +1,408 kJ/mol, so TΔS = 151 kJ/mol is absorbed.
  5. Voltages. E_rev = 1,257,000 ÷ (6 × 96,485) = 2.17 V. E_tn = 1,408,000 ÷ (6 × 96,485) = 2.43 V.
  6. Current. I = 6 × 96,485 × 0.005148 = 2,980 A.
  7. Power. P_min = 2,980 A × 2.17 V = 6.47 kW, the same as 1,257 kJ/mol × 0.005148 mol/s.
  8. Per kg. 6.47 kW ÷ 1 kg/h = 6.47 kWh/kg of aluminium.

The 1,257 kJ/mol is per mole of reaction, not per hour: it is the electrical work to make 2 mol (54 g) of aluminium. With 3 cells in series (bipolar) the stack voltage is 3 × 2.17 = 6.51 V and each cell carries 2,980 ÷ 3 = 993 A. The power is the same. Your numbers will differ slightly with the phases and temperature you choose: molten AlCl₃ at a higher temperature gives a smaller ΔG.

Monopolar And Bipolar

The wiring decides how one duty is split between current and voltage, and it is the part most often got wrong.

bipolar (series):     V_stack = N·E,   I_stack = I        high V, low I
monopolar (parallel): V_stack = E,     I_stack = N·I       low V, high I
P = V_stack·I_stack    identical either way

N is the number of cells, E and I one cell's own voltage and current. Bipolar cells sit in series: one current threads all of them and the voltages add, giving a high-voltage, low-current stack. Monopolar cells sit in parallel: each sees the same voltage and the currents add, giving the opposite. The power is identical either way — the choice is about busbars, rectifiers and sealing, not about energy.

A Worked Comparison

Aluminium smelting is the clearest example of what this mode is for. With a consumable carbon anode the reversible voltage at 960 °C is about 1.18 V; with an inert anode releasing oxygen instead it is about 2.20 V. The carbon is oxidised and contributes chemical energy, which is real and worth about a volt.

That is not the whole picture, and the mode deliberately does not pretend it is: an inert anode produces no process CO₂ and needs no replacing, which is why it is pursued despite the extra volt. What holds it back is materials that survive molten cryolite, not this number. Use the comparison to size the energy penalty, not to settle the question.

What This Does Not Do

No kinetics. There is no overpotential model, no exchange current density, no Tafel slope — those depend on electrode material, surface and age, and are in no database anywhere. The voltages here are reversible ones, and a real cell always needs more. Everything between the two is yours to supply.

It also assumes unit activity throughout: pure phases and ideal gases at the stated pressure, with no correction for concentration. For a concentrated brine or a molten-salt bath the real voltage differs by tens of millivolts, which matters for a precise energy balance and not for sizing a rectifier.
