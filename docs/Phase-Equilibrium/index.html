Phase Equilibrium

About

One chemical changes state — heated, cooled, compressed, expanded or throttled. Give the amount as a quantity for a sealed vessel or as a rate for a flowing stream; finds the final state and the heat and work.

What This Models

A substance moving from one state to another, either as a fixed amount in a vessel or piston, or as a steady stream through a device. Both are the same equation of state answering the same question; what separates them is whether you have a quantity or a rate.

Inputs

The form is three cards, Substance, Start and Process, with Calculate pinned at the bottom. Every number has its own unit menu beside it, starting in the unit chosen on the Units tab.

Substance

Single substance or Mixture is the switch at the top. For a single substance, type it in Name or formula; suggestions appear as you type, and chemicals you used recently are offered as chips. The default is Nitrogen.

For a Mixture, name each component in its own row, up to 6, with + Add component for another row and ✕ to remove one. Give each component as, a switch at the top of the mixture, says what the numbers beside the names are:

  • Mole % or Mass %: shares. Type every share but the last; the last is whatever makes 100 %, and the total reads "Total 100 % ✓" once every name and share is in. Switching between the two converts the shares you typed.
  • Amounts: each component's own amount, with the share it makes shown under it ("= 8.91 mol %"). One menu sets the unit for all of them, grouped by kind (mass, moles, volume and the four flows), and the total is their sum, so the Amount as chips and the amount field below are hidden. A volume is each component as the pure substance at the start temperature and pressure, converted to moles when you press Calculate.

Amount as is the row of chips that decides everything else. Its seven choices are Mass, Moles and Volume (a quantity, for a vessel) and Mass flow, Molar flow, Volumetric flow and Normal flow (a rate, for a stream). Type the value in the field under the chips. Switching between a quantity and a rate carries the chemical and the starting temperature and pressure across, so the form does not reset.

Start

Temperature and Pressure, side by side, starting at 20 °C and 1 bar. On a rate basis they are the inlet conditions.

Process

Held constant is the path taken: Pressure, Volume, Temperature, Adiabatic (isentropic) or Isenthalpic (throttle). Volume is offered for a quantity only. The default is adiabatic.

End point you know pins down the final state. On a quantity basis it is Final T, Final P, Final V, Heat in or Work in; on a rate basis it is Outlet Temperature, Outlet Pressure, Heat in or Work in, since a stream has no volume of its own. Type the value in the field under it. The default is a final pressure of 10 bar.

Heat in and Work in are the energy added to the fluid; type a negative value for energy taken out. On a rate basis they are a duty and a shaft power. The app then searches for the end state that takes exactly that energy: with the pressure or volume held, it searches the final temperature; with the temperature held, or for the adiabatic machine, it searches the final pressure. A pure fluid boils at one temperature, so a heat that only part-boils it has no single temperature to match; the answer is then the boiling point, with the vapour share that heat produces. Heat in rules out the adiabatic and isenthalpic paths (no heat crosses them), and Work in is offered for the adiabatic path only. If no end state can take that much energy, the app says so and asks for a smaller value.

A path that contradicts the end point you chose is greyed out rather than removed, so it stays visible why it is unavailable: a final temperature rules out holding the temperature and the isenthalpic throttle, a final pressure rules out holding the pressure, and a final volume rules out holding the volume. If your current choice becomes unavailable, it is cleared and an amber hint explains why.

Isentropic efficiency appears only for adiabatic. 100 % is the reversible ideal; a real compressor or turbine is lower.

Press Calculate. The results open on their own screen; Edit on the Inputs strip at the top takes you back to the form. Getting Around explains how the screens fit together.

The Amount Decides Which

Enter a mass, a number of moles or a volume and this is a closed system: a fixed amount in a vessel, where the work is n times the change in internal energy. Enter a mass flow, molar flow, volumetric flow or normal flow and it is an open system: a stream through a compressor, turbine, exchanger or valve, where the shaft work is the molar flow times the change in enthalpy. You do not choose between them separately — the amount you have already says which one you mean.

What Changes With It

Constant volume is offered only for a quantity. A flowing stream has no volume of its own, only a rate, so holding it constant means nothing — and for the same reason a final volume can only be asked for on a quantity basis. Everything else is common to both: constant pressure, constant temperature, adiabatic with an isentropic efficiency, and isenthalpic throttling.

The normal-flow reference defaults to 20 °C and 1 atm and is editable under Normal reference on the Units tab. Check it against your instrument: "normal" conditions are not standardised, and a 0 °C reference reads about 7 % higher in mass terms.

Method

Given the initial state and the path, the final state is solved directly from the equation of state, and the energy follows from the first law, with the sign convention that positive means energy added to the fluid.

Key Relations

n·(U₂ − U₁) = Q + W        quantity basis (closed system)
ṅ·(h₂ − h₁) = Q̇ + Ẇ        rate basis (open, steady flow)

n is the fixed amount; ṅ the flow rate. U is internal energy, h is enthalpy — the open-system form carries h rather than U because a flowing stream does flow work on its surroundings just by moving, and h = U + Pv already accounts for it. Isenthalpic (a throttle) is the special case h₂ = h₁: no work, no heat, and the temperature still changes — the Joule–Thomson effect. Combinations that contradict each other — isenthalpic against a specified final temperature, say — are greyed out rather than silently answered.

Energy and sign convention

The energy figures come from the first law, written so that positive means energy added to the fluid:

n·(U₂ − U₁) = Q + W        quantity basis (closed system: a vessel)
ṅ·(h₂ − h₁) = Q̇ + Ẇ        rate basis (open system: a stream through a device)

On the results this reads in words: Heat in is Q (or Q̇) greater than zero, Heat out less than zero; Work in is work done on the fluid (a compressor, a pump), Work out is work the fluid does (a turbine, an expanding gas). A stream carries enthalpy h rather than internal energy U, because pushing the fluid in and out of the device is itself work (P·v), and h = U + P·v already counts it.

What each process holds constant decides where the energy goes:

Held constant Heat Work What sets the final state
Pressure the answer: Q = n·(H₂ − H₁) the volume change, −P·ΔV (a stream: none) the final temperature or volume you give
Temperature Q = n·T·(S₂ − S₁), reversible the rest of the energy balance the final pressure or volume you give
Volume (quantity only) Q = n·(U₂ − U₁) none, nothing moves the final temperature or pressure you give
Adiabatic (isentropic) none the answer: W = n·(U₂ − U₁) (a stream: ṅ·(h₂ − h₁)); for a real machine the ideal work is divided by the isentropic efficiency when compressing, multiplied when expanding S₂ = S₁ at 100 % efficiency
Isenthalpic (throttle) none none h₂ = h₁; the temperature change is the Joule–Thomson effect

Worked example: nitrogen from 1 bar and 20 °C to 10 bar. Adiabatic compression reaches 290.2 °C and needs 5.67 kJ of work per mole in a closed vessel, or 7.94 kJ per mole as shaft work on a flowing stream (an ideal gas would reach 292.8 °C). Isenthalpic "compression" to the same 10 bar ends at only 21.96 °C, with no heat and no work: the 2 K rise is nitrogen's Joule–Thomson effect, about 0.22 K per bar. The two answers differ by the compression work. Isenthalpic is really the path of a gas passing through a valve from high to low pressure; run from 1 to 10 bar it is the state with the same enthalpy, not a real machine. To reach it in practice you would compress (7.94 kJ per mole in) and then cool back out the same 7.94 kJ.

What comes back

The Inputs strip at the top repeats what you asked, for example "Nitrogen · 1.0 kg · adiabatic · 1.0 → 10.0 bara".

The headline card gives what the process is about, in words rather than signs. Held pressure, temperature or volume: the heat, Heat in or Heat out. Adiabatic: the work, Work in or Work out. Isenthalpic: the final temperature, marked "enthalpy unchanged", because heat and work are both zero by definition. The same words are used for a quantity and a stream; the unit says which (kJ for a quantity, kW for a stream). Under it a sentence says what happened to the phase: "It stays gas: 20 °C → 290.2 °C", or "From liquid to gas: it crosses the boiling curve". When the crossing happens at constant pressure, the sentence adds the temperature at which it boils, "… at 99.97 °C".

Three tiles follow, always in the same places: Final phase (Outlet phase for a stream) with its vapour share, Heat, and Work. An energy that is zero by the nature of the process reads "none", with the reason under it (adiabatic, isenthalpic). Tap the ⓘ on the phase tile to see which equation of state produced it, and the ⓘ on the headline or an energy tile to open Energy and sign convention. Tap any unit on the results to show that value in another unit.

For a quantity (a vessel), the cards below are:

Card What it shows
Amount Mass and Moles: the same quantity both ways, whichever you entered.
Volume Initial volume and Final volume. The volume changes in every process except constant volume, and by orders of magnitude when a liquid boils.
End state Final temperature and Final pressure.
Phases Initial and Final phase, and the liquid / vapour split at the start and at the end.

For a stream:

Card What it shows
Flow Mass flow, Molar flow and Normal flow. Normal flow is read at your Normal reference, and is N/A for a solid, or a fluid that is not a gas at that reference.
Volumetric flow Inlet and Outlet, side by side, because for a compressor the ratio is the point and line sizing needs the outlet figure.
Outlet state Temperature and Pressure.
Phases Inlet and Outlet phase, and the liquid / vapour split in and out.

A single-phase state reads 100% / 0% or 0% / 100% for its liquid / vapour split. Anything in between means the state sits under the saturation dome, where temperature and pressure are no longer independent. A supercritical fluid reads N/A, because above the critical point there is no liquid-vapour boundary to be either side of.

For a mixture, a card for each phase present at the start or the end, Gas phase and Liquid phase, lists every component with its share of that phase and its amount, In (at the start) beside Out (at the end). The Share menu above the table switches between mol % and mass %, and the Amount menu sets the unit of the amounts. Where the mixture splits into two phases, the card's title says what share of the moles that phase holds. Where it splits into two liquids, each gets its own card, Liquid 1 phase and Liquid 2 phase, so the amounts in all the cards add up to what you entered.

Amber banners flag an extrapolated range or a mixture-model caveat, and a note names the mixture model used. The line Solved with: names the equation of state, and Methods used at the foot opens the methods sheet.

Equation Of State

The equation of state is CoolProp's reference equation where one exists for the fluid, and Peng-Robinson where none does, which is a general cubic and less accurate, especially for liquid densities. For a single substance that has a reference equation, Methods used lets you switch between Reference equation (CoolProp) and Peng-Robinson cubic; the result is calculated again with your pick. A mixture whose liquid hydrogen bonds (water with alcohols, acids or amines) is solved with an activity-coefficient model for the liquid, NRTL where fitted pair data exists and UNIFAC (marked Estimated) where it does not, with one model for the whole calculation at every temperature. Only a calculation that needs the volume to respond to pressure, holding the volume or giving a final volume, uses Peng-Robinson for that mixture, because the activity model's liquid volume ignores pressure; it then carries a Not reliable warning. Other mixtures use Peng-Robinson with fitted interaction parameters. Methods and Sources has more on the methods.

Solids And Solutions

Neither equation of state models a solid, so a chemical that is solid at your conditions is treated separately: heating or cooling it needs no equation of state, only the integral of its heat capacity. Only a final temperature can be asked for. A target pressure or volume is refused, because compressing a solid is a mechanical process this mode does not model. If the range would cross a melting point, or a sublimation or decomposition point, the calculation is refused rather than under-reporting the energy, because a latent heat would be needed that this mode has no data for. Use the Heater case, which does include melting and boiling.

A melt of salts or oxides without water (molten NaCl with KCl, for example) is shown as one liquid. Peng-Robinson has no data for such melts and would split them into two liquids, but they mix in all proportions; a warning says so, and that the energies are approximate.

Water plus dissolved solutes with fitted data is treated as an aqueous solution, from fitted density and heat capacity rather than an equation of state. It carries no phase equilibrium, so the same final-temperature-only rule applies.

Limitations

One substance or one mixture at a time, with its chemical identity fixed: nothing reacts here. For species turning into one another use Chemical Equilibrium or Reaction. Large excursions can push the fluid outside the equation of state's fitted range.
