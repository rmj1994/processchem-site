Reaction

About

Enter the reactants and products of a reaction you already know; balances it, applies your excess and conversion, and reports the outlet amounts with the heat of reaction and the equilibrium constant.

What This Models

A reaction you specify. You name the reactants and the products, and the coefficients follow from conserving every element — you never type them. You then anchor the calculation on one reference compound's amount, say how much excess the others carry, and the result is the full material balance together with the heat and the equilibrium constant.

This is the opposite of the Chemical Equilibrium mode. There you give a feed and the thermodynamics decides what forms. Here you have already decided what forms, and supply the conversion yourself. If you want to know whether your conversion is attainable rather than assume it, use Chemical Equilibrium.

Inputs

The form reads top to bottom as the equation, the reactants, the products, the inerts, the amounts and the conditions, with Calculate pinned at the bottom.

Equation

The Equation card at the top shows the balanced equation and updates as you type, with a check line under it, element by element: "✓ Balanced · Ca 1 = 1 · C 1 = 1 · O 3 = 3". A typo or a set that cannot be balanced shows straight away in amber, with the reason, rather than after you press Calculate. Half a name is the normal state of a form being filled in, so the card stays quiet until the names resolve.

If the set can be balanced more than one way, the card says so — "This set balances more than one way. Choose how the shared element divides" — and offers the pairs of products that trade against each other as chips, with a percentage for each ratio: with carbon burning to both CO and CO₂, say, "% as CO (of CO + CO₂)", the share of the carbon that ends up as CO. It appears only when the balance is genuinely ambiguous, and only for pairs that settle it.

Reactants And Products

Reactants and Products each have a card with a row per compound, up to 6 of each: a name (suggestions appear as you type) and a phase menu showing Gas, Liquid or Solid; tap it to pick another. + Add reactant and + Add product add a row; ✕ removes one. The default is calcination: one calcium carbonate solid giving calcium oxide (solid) and carbon dioxide (gas).

The compound the amount belongs to carries a KEY tag in its row. The amounts themselves are entered in the Amounts card (below).

Inerts

Anything that is present but does not react, such as the nitrogen in combustion air or a steam diluent, goes in its own Inerts card under Products, not among the reactants. Add one with + Add inert, up to 3, each with a name, a phase menu and an amount with its own unit. It passes through untouched and takes no part in the balance, but it counts toward the totals, which is what it is there for. The card is always there and empty by default.

Amounts

The Amounts card asks four questions in order, then reads the whole feed back to you.

  1. What do you know? A feed amount or A product I need (see The Two Ways To Anchor It).
  2. Key reactant and its amount (or Key product and the amount you need): pick the compound from the menu and type its amount, with its unit button. The key is a reactant for a feed you have, and a product for a production you want, because only one side can be the thing you supply. The unit menu is grouped by kind: Moles, Mass, Volume, Molar flow, Mass flow, Volumetric flow and Normal flow. Every amount on the form has its own unit, so the key can be in kg while another line is in mol; switching between an amount and a flow switches them all. A flow makes every result a rate.
  3. Other reactants: every other reactant has its own line with a menu: Stoichiometric (the default), Stoichiometric excess %, or Fixed amount (see Stoichiometric, Excess Or Fixed). Under them, Products already in the feed gives each product None in feed (the default) or Amount in feed.
  4. How much reacts? All of the limiting reactant (the default, complete conversion) or Conversion %, the share of the limiting reactant that reacts.

A summary line closes the card, for example "Feed: 1000 kmol/h CaCO₃ (key) · O₂ stoichiometric · H₂O stoichiometric + 10 % excess · limiting: CaCO₃". It says which reactant runs out first when that follows from the form. With a fixed amount it reads "limiting: found on Calculate", because only the calculation can tell.

Conditions

What do you know? comes first: Temperatures (the default, described below) or Heat added.

With Heat added you give the Inlet temperature and pressure and the heat added: a power for a flow, an energy for a quantity, negative to take heat out. Adiabatic (0) sets it to zero. The app finds the temperature at which the products leave, the one where their enthalpy exceeds the feed's by exactly the heat added. ΔH, ΔG and K are given at that temperature, and the pressure stays the inlet's. The headline is then the Outlet temperature, or the Adiabatic outlet temperature with no heat in or out. Each product keeps the phase you chose for it, so no phase change is assumed along the way. Methane burnt completely in stoichiometric air from 25 °C comes out at 2327.8 K, against the textbook 2328 K. A reaction that takes in more heat than is added would need its products colder than any data covers, and is refused with a message saying so.

With Temperatures: Reaction at gives the reaction's own Temperature and Pressure, 800 °C and 1.01325 bar by default. ΔH, ΔG and K are worked out there, and the equilibrium caveat refers to it.

Feed enters at and Products leave at each offer Reaction conditions, the default, or Other conditions, which opens a Temperature and Pressure of their own. Use them for a feed that comes in cold, or products that leave a cooler, without a separate calculation. The heat duty runs from the feed as it enters to the products as they leave. With both at the reaction conditions it is the heat of reaction alone; otherwise it includes heating the feed up and cooling or heating the products to their outlet temperature. Enthalpy depends only on the end states, so the path between does not change the duty. A feed given as a volume is read at the feed's conditions, and a product you need, given as a volume, at the products'. The out-of-phase notes check the feed and the products at their own conditions.

Press Calculate. The results open on their own screen; Edit on the Inputs strip takes you back.

The Two Ways To Anchor It

I have this feed takes the amounts you enter and works out what comes out. I need this product works backwards: you name the production you want and it sizes the feed to deliver it at your conversion. If an amount you entered outright cannot reach the target, it says so and gives the most that can be made, rather than quietly delivering less.

Stoichiometric, Excess Or Fixed

Each reactant other than the key is one of three things.

  • Stoichiometric, the default: exactly what the balanced equation needs for the key amount, so nothing of it is left over.
  • Stoichiometric excess %: a percentage on top of that. 20 % excess air means 1.2 times the oxygen the reaction needs, and 0 % is the same as stoichiometric. The excess is not consumed, so it appears in the outlet, which is what makes it visible in the masses.
  • Fixed amount: taken as entered, with its own unit. A compound can be put in short supply this way, and then it becomes the limiting reactant and the key is no longer fully converted. The result names the compound that limited it and gives its conversion; the phase tables show how much of every compound is left.

A product is either None in feed or an Amount in feed already present, such as recycled CO₂.

Cases saved before these menus existed open with every line stoichiometric, which is how they were calculated.

Results

The headline card gives the Heat duty (a power on a flow basis), with a line saying whether the reaction "Needs heat: endothermic" or "Releases heat: exothermic", and which compound was the limiting reactant. Under it an energy diagram draws the reactants and the products as two levels with ΔH between them, "energy taken in" or "energy released".

Three tiles follow: ΔH and ΔG, per mole of reaction as written at your temperature, and K_eq, the dimensionless equilibrium constant, with a word on what it means: "goes to completion", "goes partway" or "hardly goes". Tap a unit to show the value in another.

One table per phase, Gas phase, Liquid phase and Solid phase, lists the compounds in that phase, inerts included, with In (the feed) beside Out (the outlet). Each is a share of that phase and an amount, and each table has a Total row. A side with nothing in that phase shows "–" for its shares. The Share menu above each table switches between mol % and mass %; the Amount menu takes any amount or mass unit. The Whole stream card then gives the total moles and mass in and out across all phases.

The phase of each compound is the one picked on the form: Reaction does not work out phases. Where a compound is picked in a phase it would not normally be in at the feed or reaction conditions, an amber note says so and why: "H₂O is picked as liquid, but at 900 °C, 1.01325 bar it is normally a gas (it is above its critical point (373.9 °C))". The reasons are the melting point, the boiling point at the pressure, sublimation, or the critical point. The note never stops the calculation, because a "liquid" can be meant, such as a dissolved salt or a higher-heating-value basis.

The Reaction card then gives:

Row What it shows
Limiting reactant The reactant that runs out first.
Extent How many moles of reaction occurred (a molar flow on a flow basis).
ΔS (reaction) The entropy change, in J/(mol·K).
ΔH° at 298.15 K, ΔG° at 298.15 K The standard values, for checking against tables.
Conversion The conversion of the limiting reactant.

A note says what ΔH is per mole of the reference compound, that K depends on temperature alone, and whether the duty includes sensible heat. The next line names where the thermodynamic data came from, and Methods used shows the same per species. Amber banners flag anything that weakens the result: a compound entered in a phase it does not hold at your temperature, a heat capacity used beyond the range it was fitted to, a reaction whose equilibrium favours the reactants (so the conversion is yours and not one the thermodynamics allows), and gas volumes above about 10 bar, where the ideal molar volume is approximate.

Method

Each compound's enthalpy and entropy at temperature come from its standard enthalpy of formation, its absolute entropy and its heat capacity, exactly as in the Chemical Equilibrium mode. The reaction's own properties are the sums of those, weighted by the balanced coefficients νᵢ.

Key Relations

ΔH_rxn = Σ νᵢ·hᵢ            heat of reaction, products minus feed
ΔG°_rxn = Σ νᵢ·gᵢ           at standard-state activities
ln K = −ΔG°_rxn / (RT)      equilibrium constant

νᵢ is positive for a product, negative for a reactant, and the reported heat duty is this same sum applied to the whole outlet and feed: enthalpy of the outlet less enthalpy of the feed. With the feed entering at the reaction temperature the duty is the heat of reaction alone; give it its own temperature and the duty includes the sensible heat as well.

About The Equilibrium Constant

K depends on temperature alone. Pressure shifts where a real equilibrium settles, but it does not change K, and the value shown will not move when you change the pressure. Pressure does set the gas volumes.

What It Refuses

A set of compounds that cannot be balanced, or that can be balanced in more than one way where no choice of split settles it — the message says how many independent reactions the set admits, and which compound to remove. Ions are refused too: the balance conserves elements but not charge. So is a compound entered twice in the same phase, one that cancels out of the equation, or one that balances only on the other side.

Limitations

One reaction at a time, with no kinetics and no reactor sizing. Gas volumes use the ideal molar volume RT/P, which is consistent with how the activities are modelled but approximate above about 10 bar. Heat capacity correlations extrapolate silently, so a compound entered in a phase it does not hold at your temperature is flagged rather than corrected.
