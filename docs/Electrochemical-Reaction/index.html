Electrochemical Reaction

About

A reaction driven by electricity, or driving it. Balances the equation as the Reaction mode does, then reports the cell voltage, the electrical energy, the heat, and the current for a monopolar or bipolar stack.

What This Models

The thermodynamics of an electrochemical cell: what voltage a reaction needs to be driven at, or produces when it drives, and what heat goes with it. Enter the reaction exactly as in the Reaction mode and say how the cells are wired; the electrons it transfers are counted for you.

It works in both directions and tells them apart itself. A reaction with a positive Gibbs energy has to be driven — an electrolyser. One with a negative Gibbs energy drives — a battery or fuel cell. The equations are the same; only the wording changes.

Inputs

The form reads top to bottom as the set-up, the reaction, how much, the cell and the conditions, with Calculate pinned at the bottom. Most of it is the Reaction form, and behaves as described on that page. The defaults differ, opening on water electrolysis, 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂, with 1 mol/s of water fed at 25 °C.

Set-up is a picture of the cell, with the products named at their electrodes.

Equation is the balanced equation with its element-by-element check line, as on the Reaction page.

Reactants and products is folded under one row; tap it to open the compound rows, with their phase badges, + Add reactant, + Add product and, when extra amounts are on, the inerts.

How much is I have this feed or I need this product, chips to choose the reference compound where there is more than one, and its amount in a field labelled after it, such as "H₂O fed" or "H₂ produced", with a unit button. Under it, Excess or extra amounts and Not all of it reacts work as on the Reaction page.

Cell opens with the line "Electrons are counted from the oxidation states of the balanced equation." Then:

  • Cells in the stack is a whole number, at least 1. The default is 1.
  • Current efficiency is the share of the charge doing the intended reaction, in per cent, greater than 0 and at most 100. At 100 % every electron makes product. Below it, extra current is needed for the same output, so the current and the electrical power scale by 100 divided by the efficiency, while the reversible voltage does not change.
  • Wiring is Monopolar (parallel) or Bipolar (series); see Monopolar And Bipolar.

Conditions is Feed enters at and Products leave at, as on the Reaction page.

An impossible entry, such as zero cells or an efficiency of 120 %, is refused with a message saying what is wrong.

The Electrons Are Counted For You

The electrons are always worked out from the oxidation states of the balanced equation, then reported as a result. There is no field to type them in. Water balances as 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂: four hydrogens go from +1 to 0, gaining one electron each, while two oxygens go from −2 to 0, losing two each — four gained, four lost, so four transferred.

This matters because the number belongs to the equation as balanced, not to the half-reaction you remember. 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ is two electrons per molecule of hydrogen, and this equation makes two of them. Two electrons would give 2.46 V instead of 1.23 V — and 2.46 V looks perfectly ordinary, between chlor-alkali's 2.19 and magnesium's 2.48, so nothing downstream could have caught it. Counting from the equation removes that trap.

It declines rather than guessing in two cases. A reaction that moves no electrons at all — calcination, say — has none to count. And where the rules contradict themselves, as in a peroxide, where hydrogen at +1 and oxygen at −2 leave a molecule that does not add up to zero, it says so instead of returning a confident wrong number. The reaction's own result is still shown, with the message under it.

What comes back

The Inputs strip repeats the equation, the amount and the number of cells, for example "2 H₂O → 2 H₂ + O₂ · 1 mol/s water · 1 cell".

The headline card, Cell · reversible voltage, gives E_rev in volts and says which way the reaction goes: "This reaction has to be driven: it needs at least 1.229 V per cell" for an electrolyser, or "This reaction runs on its own: the cell gives up to … V" for a battery or fuel cell.

Cell voltage draws the scale between the reversible and the thermoneutral voltage, with a sentence on what happens between and beyond the two marks: for an electrolyser, between them the cell draws heat from its surroundings, and above the thermoneutral voltage the extra energy becomes heat.

Up to three tiles follow: Current (A, kA or MA) and Power (min.), the power at the reversible voltage, both only on a flow basis, and Energy per kg of the product (the reference if it is a product, else the first product), in kWh/kg, MJ/kg, kJ/kg or BTU/lb. A batch has no rate in it and so has no current or power; a note says to give the amount as a flow to get one.

The Cell card then gives:

Row What it shows
Electrons transferred The count, with the note "Counted from the equation".
Volts per electron The reversible voltage divided by the electrons. It is there as a check: a count that is double or half what the reaction needs gives a number an engineer recognises as wrong, rather than one that is merely unfamiliar.
Reversible voltage, Thermoneutral voltage E_rev and E_tn from the relations below.
Stack voltage The voltage across the whole stack, from the wiring.
Electrical energy (ΔG) Energy per mole of reaction as written.
Reversible heat (TΔS) The heat exchanged, labelled absorbed (an endothermic cell) or released.
Wiring The cell count and whether it is series or parallel, on a flow basis.

Methods used shows the method and where each compound's data came from. The reaction at the foot folds away the reaction's own result — heat duty, ΔH, ΔG and K, the Stream table and the Reaction card — exactly as on the Reaction page; tap it to open. An amber note appears if the reversible voltage falls outside the 0.5–5 V that practical cells occupy.

Method

Two relations carry everything, plus Faraday's law for the current.

Key Relations

E_rev = −ΔG/(zF)     reversible voltage — what thermodynamics demands
E_tn = −ΔH/(zF)      thermoneutral — cell neither heats nor cools
z·F·(E_tn − E_rev) = TΔS   heat exchanged, per mole reacted
I = ṅ_rxn·z·F         Faraday's law — a rate of reaction is a current

z is the electron count, F Faraday's constant, ṅ_rxn the molar rate of reaction. The heat TΔS is absorbed by an electrolyser, released by a fuel cell. Give the amount as a flow to get ṅ_rxn and so a current; a batch has no rate in it and so has no current.

Monopolar And Bipolar

The wiring decides how one duty is split between current and voltage, and it is the part most often got wrong.

bipolar (series):     V_stack = N·E,   I_stack = I        high V, low I
monopolar (parallel): V_stack = E,     I_stack = N·I       low V, high I
P = V_stack·I_stack    identical either way

N is the number of cells, E and I one cell's own voltage and current. Bipolar cells sit in series: one current threads all of them and the voltages add, giving a high-voltage, low-current stack. Monopolar cells sit in parallel: each sees the same voltage and the currents add, giving the opposite. The power is identical either way — the choice is about busbars, rectifiers and sealing, not about energy.

A Worked Comparison

Aluminium smelting is the clearest example of what this mode is for. With a consumable carbon anode the reversible voltage at 960 °C is about 1.18 V; with an inert anode releasing oxygen instead it is about 2.20 V. The carbon is oxidised and contributes chemical energy, which is real and worth about a volt.

That is not the whole picture, and the mode deliberately does not pretend it is: an inert anode produces no process CO₂ and needs no replacing, which is why it is pursued despite the extra volt. What holds it back is materials that survive molten cryolite, not this number. Use the comparison to size the energy penalty, not to settle the question.

What This Does Not Do

No kinetics. There is no overpotential model, no exchange current density, no Tafel slope — those depend on electrode material, surface and age, and are in no database anywhere. The voltages here are reversible ones, and a real cell always needs more. Everything between the two is yours to supply.

It also assumes unit activity throughout: pure phases and ideal gases at the stated pressure, with no correction for concentration. For a concentrated brine or a molten-salt bath the real voltage differs by tens of millivolts, which matters for a precise energy balance and not for sizing a rectifier.
